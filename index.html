Day 0

Almost Earth’s twin, on paper

NASA’s TESS satellite spotted TOI-700 d in 2019, and the Spitzer Space Telescope confirmed it. It was the first Earth-sized planet TESS found in a star’s habitable zone.

Its star, TOI-700, is an M dwarf: about 40% the size and mass of the Sun, and much cooler. That’s why a planet that orbits this close still gets less starlight than Earth does.

The light you’d catch from TOI-700 tonight left it around 1925. You’d need a telescope though; the star is far too faint for the naked eye.

Bars compare each value to Earth (the dark line marks Earth = 1).

Found by a tiny shadow

Nobody has seen TOI-700 d directly. TESS noticed the star dim very slightly, every 37.4 days, as the planet crossed in front of it. That dip is called a transit.

Could it hold water?

“Habitable zone” only means the starlight is right for liquid water. It doesn’t mean the planet has water, air, or life.

A planet’s basic temperature depends on how much starlight it gets and how much it reflects. Move the slider to change the reflectivity (albedo) and compare with Earth.

Earth’s number comes out below freezing too. Our real average is about 15 °C because greenhouse gases trap heat. TOI-700 d would need an atmosphere doing the same.

One side in daylight forever?

Planets this close to small stars are expected to become tidally locked, with one face always toward the star. Climate models have to work out whether winds can move enough heat to the night side.

What the models say

Suissa et al. (2020) ran 20 3D climate scenarios with different atmospheres, surfaces, and rotation. Several kept temperate conditions, but the atmospheric signals they predict are under 10 parts per million, too faint for today’s telescopes.

A neighbor in the zone

In 2023 the team announced TOI-700 e, slightly smaller than Earth and orbiting just inside d. It sits in the optimistic habitable zone.
